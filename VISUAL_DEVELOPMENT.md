# Visual development — required before polished generation

Purpose: translate a strong script into images worth watching and a sequence worth following. Readability is necessary; it is not the whole artistic goal. Record concrete visual choices and inspect their execution rather than adding generic “cinematic” adjectives.

These are craft requirements and editorial judgments, not scientifically validated retention thresholds. Use the selected genre, audience, medium, and central question. Preserve the story's causality, required narration, and satisfying resolution.

## 1. Compare distinct opening designs

For the selected premise, sketch **at least three different visual opening concepts** before polishing images. They must change the staging, reveal, scale, viewpoint, or visual relationship—not merely lens wording or color.

For each concept record:
- What is arresting in the first visible image?
- What does the viewer understand, and what do they want to discover?
- How does it expose or launch the film's central question?
- What does the following action reveal or change?
- Can it read at phone size without prior explanation?
- Can the chosen medium and available tools execute it clearly?

Possible approaches include a striking contrast of scale, a revealing action already underway, an emotionally charged relationship, a meaningful visual contradiction, or an object/detail whose context is revealed. These are options, not a checklist to force into every opening.

Reject empty spectacle, unclear geography, or an attractive mystery that withholds the basic question. Select the two strongest feasible concepts for the complete rough-sequence comparison required in SYSTEM_PROMPT.md. Label concept-only judgments provisional until playback; never claim viewer preference without testing.

## 2. Choose a story-specific memorable visual idea

Write one sentence identifying the film's visual idea: a particular relationship, silhouette, action, spatial arrangement, recurring object, or reveal that expresses its conflict or emotion. Identify at least one planned signature moment that viewers could describe specifically.

Explain how that idea advances the script. “Rich lighting,” “beautiful Christmas colors,” “3D,” and “fast camera movement” describe treatment, not a distinctive story image. Do not turn every shot into competing spectacle. Quiet acting can be memorable.

Create a reference board or concise annotated design sheet within the user's authorized scope. Fix the medium's materials, shape/line language, proportions, palette, motivated light, and performance/camera behavior. References guide look and staging; do not copy another film's protected characters or scenes. Do not spend on polished reference assets during a documents-only task.

## 3. Plan visual progression across the film

Map these four dramatic functions to actual pictures, allowing beats to combine:
- Opening promise: the visual relationship or action that creates interest.
- Development: how staging, revelations, character movement, or composition change with consequences.
- Turning point/climax: the visual expression of the important choice and its result.
- Payoff: the final image that makes the outcome and emotional meaning visible.

Track purposeful changes in distance/intimacy, scale, space, light/value/color, motion/stillness, and density where useful. Do not require all dimensions to change or assign a universal color meaning. A controlled visual contrast can be more effective than constant intensity.

Within-shot blocking, an emerging expression, a foreground/background reveal, or an eyeline can develop interest without a cut. A cut should change what viewers perceive or feel. Do not use automatic framing alternation or camera movement as substitutes for development.

## 4. Make the picture carry key story events

Identify which essential facts must be visually demonstrated: an obstacle's effect, a choice, a transfer of an object, a discovery, or a relationship change. Give each a readable action and consequence.

For each narration line, state what the picture adds. Narration may establish stakes, viewpoint, rules, or anticipation; it must not merely excuse pictures that fail to show the central action. Muted review checks visual comprehension, not whether every narrated nuance can be recovered without sound.

Show performance through specific playable behavior rather than “looks emotional.” Protect readable faces, silhouettes, contact, weight, and reaction. Simplify choreography or redesign coverage when generation cannot deliver it; preserve the story function.

## 5. Design the visual payoff alongside the opening

Specify the final image and the setup that gives it meaning. It may echo an earlier composition/object with changed meaning, reveal a consequential relationship, or complete an established action. A literal matching frame, twist, or seamless replay loop is optional.

Ask: what visibly changed, why does it matter, and when has the audience received the emotional reward? Do not add an idle farewell after the strongest ending without a purpose. Do not sacrifice a complete conclusion to create rewatch bait.

## 6. Annotate each storyboard panel

Every panel must answer:
1. **What makes this worth looking at?**
2. **What makes the viewer want the next shot?**

Support the answers with specific composition, performance, action, or revelation. For the final panel, replace the second question with: **What makes this a satisfying place to end?**

Record first focal point; the script fact/emotion conveyed; visual interest device; entry/action/response/exit; information revealed and still withheld; next-shot expectation or final reward; continuity; intended duration and cut/hold cue. If adjacent panels repeat the same information with no meaningful change, combine or redesign them.

Separate four checks: readable, visually engaging, narratively useful, and feasible. Passing one does not establish the others. Do not claim a panel is engaging solely because it is technically polished.

## 7. Review actual images and motion

Inspect opening concepts first at phone size, then the full rough sequence at normal speed with temporary sound, plus muted review. Ask an authorized fresh viewer what caught their eye, what seemed unclear or repetitive, which image they remember, and whether the ending rewarded the beginning. Record actual responses or SELF-REVIEW ONLY.

Review source clips and the final export against the same visual plan. Verify that the intended reveal, focal shift, expression, visual progression, and payoff actually occur. A correct first frame does not establish successful motion or transitions.

If visual interest depends on an unreadable detail, missing action, arbitrary spectacle, or a payoff not established earlier, revise staging or editing before treating it as ready. Taste judgments remain provisional without audience response; document disagreements rather than inventing unanimous approval.

## Performance and finish integration

Apply [PERFORMANCE_AND_FINISH.md](PERFORMANCE_AND_FINISH.md). Add consistent character/expression/key-pose references and representative color/light keyframes to the design package. Map important spoken phrases to prior knowledge, timed reveal, and ensuing reaction. Inspect actual motion, contacts, identity, and adjacent-shot design together; record material defects and repairs. Before bulk polished generation, demonstrate the selected method in the finished two-shot sample.

## Required visual-development deliverable

- Three annotated visual opening concepts and selection reasons.
- One story-specific visual idea and planned signature moment.
- A visual progression map linked to the script's turning points.
- Annotated storyboard panels with the two questions above.
- A planned opening-to-payoff relationship.
- Two opening variants compared in rough playback, or an explicit pending status.
- Observed findings, unresolved execution risks, and fresh-viewer evidence or provisional self-review.

Include this package in the default development review. Under explicit autonomous production authorization, retain it as the working plan and carry untested assumptions forward honestly. It does not authorize public release or guarantee attention, retention, or virality.

## Connected coverage and smooth rhythm

Apply [SMOOTH_EDITING.md](SMOOTH_EDITING.md) during panel and motion planning. Keep a complete physical or emotional action in one shot when that reads best; a new narrated phrase does not require a new setup. Protect meaningful preparation, contact, consequence, and reaction. Specify matching action phases and focal positions for adjacent views, with usable editing handles when feasible. Plan the emotional breathing room in the rough sequence; do not rely on slowing clips, repeating frames, or adding dissolves later. Check the connected dramatic unit as well as the two-shot join.
